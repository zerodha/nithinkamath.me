If nature has no price, does it have no value?

17 Nov 2026

I recently watched the brilliant documentary How to Live on Earth, and I think all of us should watch it.

It left me with a lot of questions, but one stuck with me: what is the value of a tree?

A lot of people would say its value is in what it becomes once it’s cut down: timber, firewood, paper. Think about the absurdity of that. The tree is worth more dead than alive.

That’s the problem with our current economic system: it’s extractive. Yes, it’s responsible for the material progress and comforts we enjoy. But we’ve never reckoned with the cost of this progress, and now the bills have come due in the form of climate change and poor air, water, and soil.

At the heart of the problem is this utilitarian, instrumentalist mindset: things only have value if you can put a price on them. If you can’t, they’re treated as worthless.

So the Amazon rainforest is worth less than the gold and minerals beneath it, and less than the land you’d get by clearing it for more lucrative crops.

We talk about the tragedy of the commons all the time now. But I don’t think we really understand how tragic the tragedy is.

Free markets have their virtues. They’re good at putting a price on what we extract from nature. But they often fail to value leaving things intact: maintaining them, conserving them. Until we change this bad calculus and start valuing things while they’re alive, not just once they’re dead, things will keep getting worse.

Which leaves me with one question: if something doesn’t have a price, does that mean it has no value?
